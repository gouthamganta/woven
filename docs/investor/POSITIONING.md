# Positioning

## The Quadrant Woven Occupies

Dating apps can be mapped across two axes: **delivery model** (infinite scroll vs. curated/limited) and **matching intelligence** (stated preferences vs. behavioral ML). The current market clusters heavily in one quadrant: infinite scroll + stated preferences. This is where Tinder, Hinge, and Bumble all live, despite their surface-level differences.

The premium-intentional quadrant — curated delivery + behavioral ML — is structurally underoccupied. Coffee Meets Bagel enters it partially on the delivery axis (daily curated) but without behavioral ML. Thursday enters it partially on the intentionality axis (temporal scarcity) but without any persistent ML layer.

Woven is built to occupy this quadrant fully: a curated daily deck driven by a 16-component behavioral ML engine that adapts per user over time.

## The Three Problems Woven Is Positioned Against

### 1. Swipe Fatigue

The dominant user complaint about dating apps — documented in multiple widely reported consumer surveys — is not that there aren't enough people on the apps. It is that the apps feel like a second job: infinite scrolling, low-conversion matches, and the cognitive burden of evaluating dozens of profiles daily with no sense that the system is learning anything.

Woven's positioning against swipe fatigue is structural, not rhetorical. The daily deck (Moments) is finite by design. There is no infinite scroll. ECHO's behavioral ML means the deck gets more relevant over time, rather than requiring users to apply more cognitive effort to compensate for a static algorithm.

The spark economy (5 sparks/day, 1 per Drawn action) creates a natural daily boundary: users engage intentionally because engagement has a cost, and that cost resets daily. This is not a paywall — it is a design decision about what kind of behavior the app rewards.

### 2. Rating Anxiety

Hinge displays compatibility percentages. Tinder implies desirability ranking through Boost visibility mechanics. Multiple apps show users how many people have liked them, creating a performance context where users optimize for platform-visible approval metrics rather than for genuine self-presentation.

Rating anxiety is the psychological cost of making the matching mechanism visible to users. When users can see their compatibility score, their like count, their desirability rank — they begin crafting profiles and behaviors to maximize those numbers. The result is corrupted training signal and a UX that feels like being evaluated, not like meeting someone.

Woven's hard design rule is explicit: no compatibility scores, no community ratings, no AI labels or badges are ever shown to users. Every AI surface is invisible UX. The brand language is "intentionality," not "optimization." Users should feel like they are being introduced thoughtfully, not scored publicly.

### 3. The Stated-Preference Illusion

Questionnaire-based matching systems (Hinge's Dealbreakers, OkCupid's questions, Bumble's preference filters) rest on a premise that behavioral economics research has consistently challenged: that people accurately know and report what they want in a partner. The evidence is that they do not. Stated preferences and revealed preferences diverge systematically.

Woven's positioning against this illusion is technical: ECHO learns from what users do, not from what they say they want. The 7-signal ConnectionScore (BalloonPopped, TrialRequested, TrialAccepted, ConversationDepth, DateAccepted, ExplicitFeedback, LoveReactions) is built from behavioral outcomes, not from questionnaire responses. Per-user weight learning (weekly logistic regression) means ECHO continuously updates its model of what actually predicts connection for each individual user.

## The Invisible AI Thesis

Woven's foundational product philosophy is: **AI is infrastructure, not a feature.**

This is a deliberate inversion of the current industry trend, where AI is increasingly made visible (compatibility percentages, AI-generated icebreakers labeled as AI, "Most Compatible" badges). Making AI visible creates the rating anxiety problem described above. It also creates a product that users can game, which corrupts the training signal.

When AI is infrastructure:
- Users experience better matches, not a dashboard showing them how they were matched
- The explanation for a match is personalized in tone (playful, calm, or serious, based on the viewer's TimeToFirstMessageMs history) but does not expose the scoring mechanism
- Date ideas (generated by Find Love) are personalized based on past accepted ideas and the tone that preceded fast first messages — but users experience this as thoughtful suggestions, not as "AI picked these for you"
- ChatNotes written by users feed future embedding generation — but users experience this as a natural expression mechanic, not as "training data collection"

The invisible AI thesis is not a marketing position. It is enforced at the code level: there is no endpoint that exposes ECHO scores to frontend users, no component that renders compatibility percentages, no notification that references algorithmic reasoning.

## How Each Product Decision Reinforces Positioning

### Daily Deck (vs. infinite scroll)

The finite daily deck signals scarcity and curation. It tells users: these are candidates we think are worth your time today. This is not random proximity matching. It creates anticipation rather than obligation, and it prevents the cognitive fatigue of infinite scroll.

Technically, the deck composition is driven by ECHO scoring + LinUCB bandit ordering, which means it improves as behavioral data accumulates. The positioning benefit is that users experience increasing deck quality over time as a natural product behavior, not as a visible algorithm tuning itself.

### Spark Economy (vs. free-for-all)

The spark economy (5/day, 1 per Drawn action, 0.5 ghost refund) is a behavioral gate. It filters the user base toward intentional engagement: users who are willing to spend a spark on a candidate are signaling a higher level of interest than a frictionless right swipe. The ghost refund preserves perceived fairness — if a match ends with no engagement, the spark is partially returned.

This positions Woven as a platform that values the effort of matching as much as the act of matching. The positioning message: your attention here means something.

### Trial Period (vs. permanent ghosting)

The 3-minute mutual trial window creates a structured commitment checkpoint after a match forms. Both users must signal intent to continue (TrialAccepted) before the full connection (Find Love) unlocks. This addresses the most cited post-match problem in existing apps: the match that forms and immediately goes silent.

The trial period positions Woven as a platform with explicit connection mechanics, not implicit hope. It also generates a high-quality behavioral signal (TrialAccepted carries 0.25 weight in ConnectionScore) that makes ECHO's outcome labels more accurate.

### ChatNotes (vs. one-tap expression)

Requiring a note (20-150 characters) when making a choice on a Moments card is an effort gate and a signal surface. It positions Woven against the one-tap culture of pure swipe apps. The note requirement says: if you're going to act on this, say something about why.

Notes are never shown to other users — this is a hard design rule. They are background signal, not feedback. The positioning benefit is that users feel the weight of their choices without the anxiety of being judged on their note quality by the person they chose.

| Term | How Woven uses it |
|---|---|
| Intentionality | The design philosophy: finite deck, spark gate, trial period, notes — all create friction that rewards intentional users |
| Behavioral | What ECHO learns from — revealed actions, not stated preferences |
| Invisible AI | AI as infrastructure: no scores, no labels, no badges — only better matches |
| Curated | The daily deck is selected, not served at random — ECHO makes the selection, not a human editor |
